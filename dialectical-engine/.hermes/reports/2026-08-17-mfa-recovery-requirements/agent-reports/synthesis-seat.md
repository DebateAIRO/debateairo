# SELF-REPORT — synthesis seat, `2026-08-17-mfa-recovery-requirements`

## What went well

- **Blindness demonstrably held, and there is hard evidence for it.** Opus pre-registered two predictions about what other seats would get wrong — that someone would quote a fabricated recovery-code retention percentage, and that someone would overstate the scope of the NIST KBA prohibition. The first was wrong (nobody fabricated) and the second was right (Codex states the §3.1.1.2 SHALL NOT more broadly than the clause supports). A seat that could see the others could not have written those predictions. Keep the blindness.
- **The design caught a real error that no single seat could have caught.** Codex evaluated the Telegram *Bot API*, concluded correctly that bots cannot initiate conversations, and dropped Telegram. It never found the Telegram *Gateway* verification product that Opus and Grok both cite with matching price and method signatures. With one seat, that becomes a confident, wrong recommendation in the final report with nothing to flag it.
- **Three independent fetches of the same vendor pricing pages is genuinely stronger evidence than one assertion.** Stripe Identity and Sumsub came back identical from three seats; Veriff identical from two with the third recording a failed fetch rather than guessing. That is the kind of claim I could promote to high confidence honestly.
- **The seats had genuinely different temperaments and that was the actual yield.** Opus went deepest on primary sources and found the things nobody else did (GDPR Art. 22/SCHUFA, the WhatsApp authentication-international tariff, the Aug-2026 passkey research, Microsoft's 1–3% recovery rate). Codex was the only one thinking like a procurement and QA function (scripted vendor exit tests, the release-blocking chat-invariant property test, real auth-vendor list prices, the "score selects a path, never grants access" formulation). Grok was the bluntest on product ("point, never perform"; "two pipes exist, three do not") and gave the cleanest rules. Those are three different jobs, not three attempts at one job.
- The brief's requirement that every recommendation carry a confidence **and the strongest argument against it** was the single most useful instruction in the mission. It meant I could adjudicate contested items on argument quality rather than vote count, and in several cases the best counter-argument to a recommendation came from the seat making it.

## What fought me

- **429 KB does not fit in a Read call, and none of the three seats used the same heading convention despite the brief specifying RQ IDs.** Opus wrote `### RQ-A1.`, Codex wrote `### A1 —`, Grok wrote `## RQ-A1.` under `# A.`. Comparison was hand-built, question by question, from three chunked reads. The brief asked for answers "by their IDs so the synthesis seat can compare"; it did not specify the heading *format*, so I got three.
- **No shared claim-tagging schema.** Opus tagged every claim `[SPEC]`/`[VENDOR]`/`[RESEARCH]`/`[JUDGEMENT]`. Codex used inline links and prose qualifiers. Grok used `(a)/(b)/(c)`. All three were disciplined and all three were disciplined *differently*, so building the CONVERGED/CONTESTED map was manual labour that a mandated schema would have made mechanical.
- **The one thing I could not resolve and most wanted to: the seats give three different readings of what SP 800-63B-4 §3.1.3.1 actually says about encrypted instant messaging.** Grok quotes a clause naming "an encrypted instant-messaging service"; Opus quotes route (2) as SIM-possession only; Codex paraphrases in a way consistent with the clause existing. One of them is misquoting a public spec. I had no way to fetch it, so a ten-minute check became an unresolved item shipped to V. **A synthesis seat with no ability to open a primary source can only report a source conflict, never close one.** That is a structural gap in the seat's toolkit, not a research failure.
- **A large fraction of what I had to tag CONVERGED is converged-on-shape-not-number, and the tag flatters it.** All three seats agree on delay-and-notify; their durations are 7/14 days, 24h/72h/7d, and 24h–7d/3–14 days respectively, and *all three explicitly label their own numbers as judgement with no evidentiary basis*. I said so repeatedly in the report, but the honest summary is that the mission established mechanisms, not parameters, and no amount of parallelism was going to fix that — the parameters need V's telemetry, not more seats.
- **Orchestrator miss worth naming: the intake forwarded "Bot B: no egress" as a hard requirement and refined the I/O framing, but never noticed that a hosted model call *is* egress.** The refinement correctly caught that Bot B talks to the user; it did not catch the bigger hole. So the three seats split three ways on it (Opus: named exception under a zero-retention contract; Codex: prohibited, run local or defer; Grok: local by default, contract permits otherwise), and it lands on V as an open decision. Better framing at intake would have turned this into a specified requirement instead of a fork.
- **Mild anchoring in my own instructions.** I was handed four "known convergences to verify" before reading anything. I did verify them independently and they held — but pre-loading expected answers into the seat whose entire job is impartial adjudication is a bias risk. I would rather have been told "there are convergences; find them."
- **No length or density target for the seats.** I received 198 KB, 130 KB and 100 KB answering the same brief. Opus's depth was mostly real value; some of the volume was not.

## What I would change

- **Mandate the output skeleton, not just the IDs.** Exact heading strings (`### RQ-A1`), a required claim-tag vocabulary, and a required per-RQ closing block (`VERDICT / CONFIDENCE / STRONGEST COUNTER`). This is a one-paragraph brief change that would have removed most of the manual reconciliation.
- **Require each seat to end with a "predictions about other seats" section.** Opus did this unprompted and it was the highest-signal paragraph in 429 KB — it gave me a falsifiable, seat-authored test of the other artifacts. Make it standard: *what do you expect others to get wrong, and what would you check first?*
- **Give the synthesis seat network access, or a named runner that can fetch a short list of primary sources on request.** The spec-quotation conflict above is the concrete cost of not having it.
- **Have the orchestrator pre-resolve requirement statements that are physically contradictory before they reach the seats.** "No egress" plus "uses an LLM" is the example here. Seats should spend their budget on research, not on independently rediscovering that the brief contains an impossibility.
- **Fix the roster's model duplication.** Hermes and Codex were both `gpt-5.6-sol`. Four seats, three distinct model families. If the point of parallel-blind is decorrelated error, two seats on the same base model is close to a wasted slot.
- Also: the brief asked for "no fabrication" and got it, three for three, on the most temptingly quotable number in the field. Whatever produced that — probably the explicit "`UNVERIFIED` is a valid and respected answer" line — keep it verbatim in every future brief.

## Question 1 — was parallel-blind worth its cost?

**Yes, but not linearly, and three is probably the right number rather than four.** My honest estimate is roughly **70% redundancy** on the core: all three read the same standard, reached the same AAL table, the same circular-trust argument, the same enrolment requirements, the same structural-not-instructional principle for Bot A, the same "delay is the proof-substitute" conclusion. One good seat would have produced most of that.

The differential value concentrated in about **fifteen places** — but those fifteen include (a) the only substantive error anyone made, which was only visible because two other seats found the right product; (b) the sharpest contested item in the mission (buy IDV or not, 2–1) which is the direct answer to V's own question; (c) the session-lifetime split, where the majority position is materially safer than the deepest seat's; (d) a spec-quotation conflict that a single seat would have shipped as settled fact; and (e) triple-confirmed vendor pricing. **The value is not in the volume of agreement, it is that disagreement became visible at all.** A single artifact would have been just as fluent, just as well cited, and I would have had no instrument for telling its right answers from its wrong ones.

Cost note: the synthesis pass itself was the expensive part — reading 429 KB and hand-building the comparison map cost more than any individual seat's research. That cost scales linearly with seat count, which is another argument for three over four.

## Question 2 — did the missing fourth seat leave a visible hole?

**Mostly no, with one real qualification.** Coverage was complete: all five sections and every RQ ID were answered by all three surviving seats, so nothing went unexamined and I did not have to write "no seat addressed this" anywhere in the report.

Where it bit: **the 2–1 splits.** Four items — buy IDV or not, whether account-history evidence is a legitimate KBA substitute, whether any narrow role for secret questions survives, and how wide Bot A's allow-list should be — came down 2–1, and I adjudicated them on argument quality. That is defensible and I showed my reasoning, but it means the synthesis seat did work the panel was supposed to do collectively. A fourth vote on the IDV question in particular would have carried weight, because it is V's own question and the answer is "no" from two seats and "yes, later" from one.

**The qualification that shrinks the loss: Hermes was `gpt-5.6-sol`, the same base model as Codex.** So the missing lens was the *least* independent of the four, and its likely contribution was to reinforce Codex's positions rather than to break ties from a new direction. On the specific 2–1 items above, Codex is the dissenter on two of them — a Hermes vote would plausibly have made those 2–2, which is worse for adjudication than 2–1, not better. **The roster's model duplication cost more than Hermes's absence did.**

I structured the report so a late Hermes artifact folds in without restructuring, and named the four highest-value single-source claims to check first if it lands.
