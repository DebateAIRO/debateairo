Second, the access goal is better served by means-tested and age- or enrolment-based passes at zero or near-zero cost: these reach the riders actually deterred by price without paying for the many riders who were already willing to pay, so universal free fares buy equity expensively and indiscriminately. Third, the claimed reduction in car traffic is the weakest link. In a mid-sized city without severe congestion or parking scarcity, the elasticity of car use to a zero bus fare is small; most of the new ridership is induced demand — students, shoppers, off-peak discretionary trips — not diverted drivers, and those extra peak loads arrive without added capacity, producing crowding and slower running that pushes out the commuters transit most needs to keep. That directly collides with the opponent's own condition that frequency must not decline: keeping frequency while removing fares means replacing the lost revenue and then also paying for the extra capacity the new riders require, so the position funds its benefits with money it has just stopped collecting. The sharper package is targeted free or near-free passes for low income, youth, disability and school enrolment, off-peak discounts to flatten the peak, off-board and contactless payment to cut dwell time, and a ring-fenced share of the transit budget for frequency and reliability. On equity, this reaches the cost-constrained riders more precisely; on ridership, it improves the service attributes that actually determine whether people choose the bus; on fiscal grounds, it keeps a stable funding base instead of gambling it on a dedicated source.
