# R-A — Adversarial Design Review of Crypto-Shredding (Wave 2 §9.2)

Blind research seat, 2026-08-19. Read-only. Retrieval date for all web sources: 2026-08-19.

## VERDICT: **WOUNDED** — fixable, but not as specified

Directionally right; could not be killed. But **wounded in the one place that decides whether it works at all: the shred point is inside the thing that is never deleted.** `identity.user_data_key` lives in the same Postgres cluster that §19 backs up, that Wave-3 restore drills restore, and that **DR-188(3) declares permanent at the datadir level**. §19's load-bearing sentence — *"backups contain ciphertext only"* — is **false**: a `pg_dump` or datadir copy contains the wrapped DEK **and** the ciphertext. Destroying a live row while fifteen immortal archives each hold a copy is not erasure; it is erasure-shaped. **Anyone holding the KEK plus any archive reverses every past shred, forever — and DR-188 guarantees the archives exist.**

The design is right in its *choice*, wrong in its *placement*.

## FRONT 1 — LEGAL: WOUNDED (honestly hedged, but over-claimed in prose)

**Retrieved:** EDPB **Guidelines 01/2025 on Pseudonymisation** (46pp, full text extracted) · CJEU **C-413/23 P EDPS v SRB** press release (4 Sep 2025) · EDPB Guidelines 5/2019 (scope check) · GDPR Art. 4/17/Recital 26 via **mirror** (gdpr-info.eu).
**FAILED (UNVERIFIED):** EUR-Lex CELEX:32016R0679 (empty body to WebFetch *and* curl) · AEPD–EDPS *10 Misunderstandings* (403/500) · ICO right-to-erasure guidance (403). **Every GDPR quotation is mirror-sourced, one step short of primary.**

- **1a. The text does not forbid crypto-shredding.** Art. 4(2) lists *"…restriction, erasure or destruction"*; neither Art. 4 nor Art. 17 defines what erasure technically requires. **SURVIVES on the text.**
- **1b. But EDPB is on record that key destruction ≠ anonymity.** Guidelines 01/2025 ¶22 verbatim: *"Even if all additional information retained by the pseudonymising controller has been erased, the pseudonymised data becomes anonymous only if the conditions for anonymity are met."* ¶86 puts encryption keys inside the Art. 4(5) "additional information" category. So destroying the DEK gets you to *"the controller erased the additional information"* — **not** to *"this is no longer personal data."* The §9.2 hedge is correct; the surrounding prose over-claims.
- **1c. ¶21 is the one that should worry you** — verbatim: *"Additional information may also exist beyond the immediate control of the pseudonymising controller… information from publicly accessible sources… may contribute to the attribution."* This provision eats a public-repo leak.
- **1d. C-413/23 P cuts both ways.** Helps: *"pseudonymised data must not be regarded as constituting, in all cases and for every person, personal data."* Hurts: *"the identifiable nature of the data subject must be assessed at the time of collection… and from the point of view of the controller."* For **you**, holding the KEK, the ciphertext *is* personal data until the DEK dies. Also: the CJEU **set aside and referred back** — principle confirmed, facts unsettled; and the case concerns Reg. 2018/1725, analogous but not identical. **UNVERIFIED as applied.**
- **1e. The public-repo question, corrected.** Wave 1 H1 is **not a ciphertext leak** — it is 1,731 files of **plaintext** datadir with verbatim `raw_artifact.raw_text`. **Crypto-shredding does nothing for it.** That is a live breach-notification question, not a key-management one. But for *future* leaks the answer changes catastrophically: **a datadir copy carries the ciphertext and the wrapped DEK in the same artifact** — the two halves the design keeps apart travel together. With ¶21, one future datadir push makes the pseudonymisation ineffective for every user in it, and DR-188(3) forbids deleting the archive.
- **1f. A trap to keep out of §24.** Vendor/blog content claims *"EDPB (Guidelines 5/2019), the UK ICO and CNIL all recognize crypto-shredding as valid erasure."* Checked: **Guidelines 5/2019 is "on the criteria of the Right to be Forgotten in the search engines cases" — search-engine delisting only, no discussion of key destruction.** The claim is **false as to its only named authority.** ICO/CNIL unreachable — **UNVERIFIED**, assert neither way.
- **1g.** No retrievable regulator opinion endorsing crypto-erasure as Art. 17 erasure was found. Absence of evidence only — four authorities were unreachable. Re-run this front from a seat with working access.

## FRONT 2 — CRYPTOGRAPHIC: WOUNDED, with an internal contradiction that is a design bug

- **2a. KEK leak = total compromise *and retroactive un-erasure*.** One key, one file, one level. A KEK leak in 2028 exposes users shredded in 2026 who were told their data was gone — the difference between a breach and a false statement to a data subject.
- **2b. Per-user DEK is the wrong granularity, and the document contradicts itself.** §9.3 rejects per-debate keys ("N-key ceremony, partial-failure states") — but §11 grants *"Delete own private debate | Owner + step-up (shreds its content key)"* and §15 says *"User deletes one debate → Content key shredded"*. **There is no per-debate content key in §12.2.** With a flat per-user DEK, deleting one private debate is **unimplementable** — and V's Q12 ruling explicitly requires it. **Fix: three levels — `KEK → user DEK → per-debate content key (wrapped by the user DEK)`.** "Delete one debate" = destroy one wrapped row; "delete everything" = destroy the user DEK, transitively killing every content key — **one atomic act, no N-key ceremony**. §9.3's rejection should be reversed.
- **2c. The HMAC blind index — construction blessed, specification missing.** EDPB ¶89/¶107 explicitly bless keyed one-way functions, so the *choice* survives. But: **small-domain enumeration** (an attacker with DB read **plus the HMAC key** confirms any address offline for the whole user base); it **cannot** be per-user (defeats lookup) so it is necessarily **one global key** that must be **separate from and independently rotatable from the KEK**; **rotation is not free** contra §12.2 — re-keying the index requires decrypting every email, and **shredded users' index rows can never be re-keyed** (so the old key can never be retired unless they're dropped); and **does the index survive erasure?** If `identity.user` is retained, `email_blind_index` is a stable, deterministic, attacker-recomputable pointer to a specific human's email **outliving an Art. 17 request**. Must be stated explicitly.
- **2d. Nothing is bound to its row — and append-only makes this WORSE.** No AEAD, no associated data, no nonce discipline, no key-id in the envelope. `reject_mutation()` blocks UPDATE/DELETE but **permits INSERT** — exactly the primitive a relocation attack needs. Attacks: **(1) shred evasion by relocation** — copy a ciphertext blob from a private row into a *published* row, where it is protected by the never-dying corpus key, making it permanently unshreddable; **(2) cross-debate resurrection**; **(3) silent key-rotation breakage**. **Required: AEAD (XChaCha20-Poly1305 or AES-GCM-SIV) with associated data = (schema, table, PK, run_id, owner_user_id, key_id, envelope_version)**, key_id/version in the envelope. AD binding is nowhere in the design and is the part that stops relocation.
- **2e. Survives cleanly:** DEK leak correctly scoped to one user; the DEK-A-cannot-read-DEK-B acceptance test is right; **"encrypt at insert time genuinely preserves append-only" is sound and could not be broken.**

## FRONT 3 — OPERATIONAL: WOUNDED, and one attack is specific to *this* codebase

- **3a. The KEK is readable by the same UID that runs prompt-injectable LLM subprocesses.** Wave 1 S5: subprocesses inherit `{...process.env}`; codex `read-only` blocks writes **not reads**; grok demonstrably regained tool use; CONT-01's empty cwd **is not a filesystem jail**. The API must read the KEK file, so it runs as a UID with read access — and the relays run as that same UID. **A successful injection that regains file read is one `cat` from the root of the entire erasure guarantee.** §14's "can no longer reach secrets" is untrue while this holds. **Fix: separate unprivileged UID for relays with no read on the secret store; `KEK_PATH` excluded from every child env; ideally wrap/unwrap behind its own process on a unix socket so the KEK never enters the API address space.** Cheapest, highest-value operational change.
- **3b. "No KMS at this scale" — right conclusion, wrong axis.** §12.6 argues from staff count; a KMS actually buys **non-extractability, key-use audit, survivable escrow** (file scores 0/0/"V has a copy"). But a managed KMS on one Hetzner box just means network creds in the same file store — **so the conclusion holds for reasons not given.** Do instead: process/UID isolation + hardware-backed escrow.
- **3c. What §12.4 actually achieved.** True and valuable for exactly one threat: **storage exfiltration without host compromise** — the threat that *already materialised* (H1). Credit due. But against **host compromise** the gain is ~zero (the app unwraps on every read). And if KEK escrow ever lands in the same backup set/bucket/repo as the DB, the gain drops to zero for backup theft too. **Required: a hard written rule — the KEK never shares a backup set, storage account, or repo with the database — plus a CI secret-scanning rule for its fingerprint.** H1 happened because a name-prefix ignore rule missed one directory.
- **3d. Yes — there is a scenario where the operator loses everything, and it violates DR-188.** One KEK, one sealed copy, one human, zero drills to date. Losing it destroys the entire corpus **including the archived research corpus DR-188(4) names by hand**. Crypto-shredding **introduces a novel way to violate DR-188 — accidental global shred** — booked in §25 R3 as "requires discipline". **Discipline is not a control at n=1. Fix: Shamir 2-of-3 (V + hardware token + sealed geographically separate copy), with *reconstruction* drilled quarterly, not merely restore.**
- **3e. `DELETE` is not physical erasure — the design never says so.** MVCC dead tuple until VACUUM; WAL until segments recycle; replicas; `pg_dump`s; PITR windows. **A shred receipt issued while the wrapped DEK is recoverable from `pg_waldump` is a false statement to the data subject** — materially worse than not offering erasure. Required: `VACUUM FULL`/`pg_repack` on the key table in the shred ceremony; a **bounded, disclosed** WAL/PITR window as the true erasure latency; encrypted physical volume so residue is at least KEK-gated.

## FRONT 4 — THE PUBLICATION RE-KEY: **FAILS as specified** (repairable)

- **4a. "Re-encrypted" is impossible; only "dual-written" is buildable.** The carriers are append-only — you cannot UPDATE a row to swap user-DEK ciphertext for corpus-key ciphertext. The only available mechanism is **insert a corpus-key copy alongside, leaving the user-DEK copy forever**. §15 says "re-encrypted"; the architecture permits only dual-write. **Different systems; say which.**
- **4b. Unpublish-then-delete cannot work. Ever.** §15 books it as a counsel question; it is a **technical impossibility** under a shared corpus key — removing one published debate would require destroying the key for the entire public corpus. Unpublish hides a projection while **your own database keeps a decryptable copy under a key you will never destroy**. **Fix: per-publication key wrapped by the corpus KEK**, mirroring 2b. Then it is one row deletion and the counsel question becomes a real policy choice.
- **4c. Third-party personal data in a published debate is unerasable by anyone — a FAIL, not a wound.** User A publishes a debate containing B's personal data (§24 items 9/10 concede this will happen); A deletes their account; A's DEK dies; **the corpus-key copy remains fully readable forever**. B — never a user, unauthenticatable — files an Art. 17 request. **No key to destroy short of the whole corpus, no row to delete (append-only), no owner left to ask.** Not booked in §25. Per-publication keys turn this from unanswerable into a one-row operation.
- **4d. "The author link is severed" is not true.** Three surviving links: **(1) `core.run.owner_user_id`** — §11 adds it to an append-only table and §12.1 classifies ids as **C4 plaintext**; it cannot be deleted or nulled, so every published debate of a deleted user carries the same identifier and **the whole public corpus can be partitioned by author** (legacy `asker_id` does the same). **(2) `identity.audit_event`** — append-only *and hash-chained*, 24-month retention "then key-shredded": under the user's DEK it **breaks the chain's evidential value**; under a separate audit key the **actor→run link survives deletion for up to 24 months**. The design cannot have both and has not chosen. **(3) the shred-evidence record** must name the user to be proof. **Fix: encrypt `owner_user_id` under the user DEK plus a per-run random join surrogate; rule explicitly on audit retention vs severance.**
- **4e.** §16 promises export of "everything under their key" — after publication the content is under the *corpus* key. Define the export path to reach both, or the promise is wrong.

## FRONT 5 — THE CONSTRAINT: real for content, **manufactured for identity data**

- **5a. "DR-188 forbids deletion" overstates V's ruling — per this mission's own research.** `research/S2-personal-data-deletion.md:83-89` states: *"DR-188 names no individual tables — it governs at the migration and datadir level. Table immutability comes from `core.reject_mutation()`, independently."* None of DR-188's four consequences says "no row may ever be deleted." §9.2 conflates a V ruling with an engineering trigger, and that conflation is what makes crypto-shredding look like the **only** option.
- **Alternative A — narrow `reject_mutation()`** (grant DELETE, never UPDATE, on defined C3 carriers via a `shred` procedure under a dedicated role, with the *fact* of deletion recorded append-only). Honours DR-188 literally. Real deletion is unambiguously erasure — no EDPB ¶22 problem, no KEK-leak-reverses-erasure, no MVCC-residue-in-principle, ~60% less code. V's own DR-174-A (*"HIDDEN, NOT PRUNED"*) is a weaker objection than §9.3 implies. **Verdict: better legally, worse on product, and it does NOT solve the archive problem either.** Competitive — deserves an explicit on-record rejection, not the strawman in §9.3.
- **Alternative B — separate the personal store from the debate store.** **For C3 content this FAILS, and Wave 1 proves it:** H4 measured the question empirically embedded *inside model output* (33+35/151 raw artifacts, 24/64 restatements, 11–16/64 claims). **You cannot separate the personal data from the corpus, because the corpus *is* the personal data.** This is the strongest justification for crypto-shredding in the whole document and §9.2 under-uses it. **For C2 identity data B is STRICTLY BETTER and the design missed it:** email, recovery email, phone, pseudonym map, risk signals all live in `identity`, **already mutable** — just `DELETE` them. Encrypting deletable data under a shreddable key buys nothing, adds a KEK dependency to the login path, and creates the blind-index rotation problem.
- **The hybrid nobody steelmanned — and the seat's recommended answer:** **real DELETE for C2 + per-debate/per-publication crypto-shredding for C3 only + the shred point held outside the archived cluster.** Smaller than the current design, legally stronger (part of the erasure needs no counsel opinion), and the only variant in which §11's per-debate deletion and unpublish-then-delete are buildable. **Could not be broken on any of the five fronts.**

## The strongest remaining objection

The design is basically right — crypto-shredding is the correct tool for C3, Wave 1 H4 proves it, and the document is more honest about its costs than most architecture. But: **crypto-shredding converts a legal risk into an operational one, and this operation has n=1.** Today's failure to erase is a remediable compliance exposure. Tomorrow's design makes erasure depend on one human never losing a key, never co-locating a backup, never letting an archive escape, and running a residue ceremony correctly every time — with no dual control (§25 R2). **Deletion is verifiable by anyone; crypto-shredding is verifiable only by whoever holds the key.** The shred test proves the mechanism on a clean database; **it cannot prove that no reachable copy of the wrapped DEK exists anywhere** — and in a system whose founding law says archives are permanent, that is precisely the thing you would most want proved.
