# Keeping a public debate after its author closes the account: legitimate-interests assessment

Date: 16 November 2026. Draft for counsel; not reviewed. The legal points are written from memory and none was read
in the primary text for this draft, so every citation is [unverified] until counsel confirms it. Privacy Policy
section 4 (the publishing row) relies on this paper.

## What we do

When a person closes their account, the debates they made public stay public. The page shows "a contributor" in place
of their pseudonym, and the account record, with the email address and everything else that says who they are, is
deleted. Other people can go on digging into the debate and having reports written on it.

## Is the text that stays personal data at all?

Mostly not. A question such as "Should cannabis be legal?" and the AI-written debate under it say nothing about the
person who asked. With no name and no link to an account, that text is outside data-protection law (GDPR recital 26)
and this assessment is not needed for it.

It is needed for what is left:

- a text in which the author wrote something about themselves ("as a nurse in Piatra Neamț, I…");
- a text someone can still tie to the author by other means: a copy of the page made while the pseudonym was shown, or
  the author having told people which pseudonym was theirs;
- the pseudonym itself while it is still inside the stored copy (see the re-identification analysis).

## 1. The interest

- **The people who built on the debate.** They spent their own credit to dig into it or to have a report written. If
  the first author could remove it, their work and their money would go with it.
- **Readers.** A public debate is quoted and linked to. A page that can vanish at one person's wish is not a record
  anyone can rely on.
- **Ours.** The service is a growing body of checked public debates. It is what the Free plan gives in exchange for
  being free.

These are real and lawful interests, and the first two are other people's, not only ours (GDPR Art. 6(1)(f) covers the
interests of third parties).

## 2. Is keeping the text needed for it?

Yes. Each lighter choice fails the interest:

- Removing the whole debate destroys the contributions built on it.
- Removing only the first author's question leaves answers to nothing.
- Hiding it from the public and keeping it for contributors is not a public record.

What we can do, and do, is keep the least: the name goes, the account goes, and any personal detail in the text is
removed on request while the rest stays.

## 3. The balance

On the person's side: they may regret a question; they may fear being recognised; the page can be copied and indexed.

What weighs against that:

- **They knew.** The page says before they ask (Free) or before they publish (paid) that the debate is public and
  stays public, and the Terms say that erasing the account does not remove it. Nothing here would surprise them.
- **They are not named.** The pseudonym was random, never their own name, and it is no longer shown.
- **They were told not to include personal details**, on the page and in the Terms (section 7).
- **They keep a way out for what matters.** Personal details about them are removed when they ask. They can object to
  the whole text being kept, and each objection is weighed on its own facts (below).
- **Sensitive subjects.** A debate may be on health, religion or politics. The subject of a question is not data about
  the asker once the name is gone. Where the text itself reveals something sensitive about its author, that part is
  removed on request.

Result: for a text with no personal details and no name, the interests of contributors and readers prevail. For a
text that still says something about its author, they prevail only after that part has been removed.

## How an objection is handled (GDPR Art. 21)

1. The person writes to privacy@dezbatere.ro. No account is needed.
2. We ask what in the text relates to them and why keeping it harms them.
3. Personal details are removed or corrected at once, without weighing. This settles most cases.
4. If they still want the whole debate gone, we weigh: what harm they describe; whether anyone has built on the debate
   (if nobody has, there is little on our side and we remove it); whether removing a part would be enough.
5. We answer within one month, say what we decided and why, and name the supervisory authority.
6. We keep a short record of the request and the decision.

## What this paper does not cover

- An order from a court or an authority: it is followed.
- A person named in a debate who is not a user: Privacy Policy section 11.
- An author's rights in their own words (copyright): Terms section 9.
- Quebec: the right to have dissemination stopped (Act respecting the protection of personal information in the
  private sector, s. 28.1) has its own test, serious injury weighed against the public's interest; the steps above
  serve for it too.
